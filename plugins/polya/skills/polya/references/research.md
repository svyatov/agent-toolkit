# Research basis

Researched 2026-10-17. This is an original tutoring prompt informed by the sources below, not an imported skill or a reproduction of the book.

## Source findings

1. George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1957 edition), [checklist transcribed by Harvard Mathematics](https://people.math.harvard.edu/~knill/teaching/math21b2001/polya/index.html), gives four stages: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry out the plan, and look back. It asks whether the conditions are sufficient or contradictory, suggests related and simpler problems, calls for checking each step, and ends with independent verification and reuse of the method.
2. Pólya's discussion of teaching, [book scan hosted by the University of Hawaiʻi](https://www.math.hawaii.edu/home/pdf/putnam/PolyaHowToSolveIt.pdf), Part I, pp. 1-2 and 20-23, advocates leaving the learner a reasonable share of the work, moving from general to specific help, and avoiding rigid mechanical questioning.
3. The Institute of Education Sciences, [*Improving Mathematical Problem Solving in Grades 4 Through 8*](https://ies.ed.gov/ncee/wwc/Docs/PracticeGuide/mps_pg_052212.pdf), Recommendation 2, pp. 17-21, recommends monitoring and reflection, learner explanations, and a manageable number of prompts rather than an exhaustive checklist.

## Design decisions

The skill keeps Pólya's four stages and adds explicit distinctions between facts and assumptions, a success criterion, and checks suited to non-mathematical problems. These are adaptations, not claims about his exact wording.

Three candidate approaches come from the user's requested format, not from a fixed rule in Pólya's method. The skill asks for genuinely different approaches and admits when fewer are viable. One question per turn, progressively stronger hints, and resuming from the user's existing work make the tutor interactive without forcing the full checklist into every response.

The IES evidence concerns school mathematics. Applying these ideas to general-purpose AI tutoring is an extrapolation, not evidence that this prompt improves learning. Model smoke checks can show whether the instructions are followed in sampled conversations; they cannot establish educational effectiveness.
